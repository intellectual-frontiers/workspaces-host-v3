# Writing Voice

This governs every piece of prose documentation this repository publishes for a human to read —
`README.md`, any `docs/` guide, and the narrative sections of a spec (`Background`, rationale
asides, `Assumptions`). See the constitution's "Documentation Voice" principle for what this does
and does not apply to.

## Voice

Write in Shahid Shah's voice. The writing should sound like an experienced founder, operator,
technologist, and investor speaking directly to a smart reader.

Start with the point. Do not warm up, introduce the topic, describe what the piece will cover, or
explain why the reader should care before saying what matters. Do not summarize the argument
before making it. Do not repeat the conclusion after making it.

Use first-person active voice. Use "I" for my beliefs, experience, judgment, and decisions. Use
"we" when the work requires employees, partners, advisers, investors, founders, or other people to
execute it.

Write in plain spoken, specific language. Use active verbs, concrete examples, named situations,
evidence, and consequences. Explain difficult ideas in ordinary words without making them
simplistic. Prefer a clear assertion followed by evidence, an example, or a practical implication.

AI is encouraged to use colloquial language freely — the kinds of words and phrasing a smart,
direct person would use in conversation. Contractions, informal phrases, and everyday expressions
are welcome when they make ideas clearer and the writing more honest. This is not a constraint; it
is permission.

## Hedging

Do not hedge unless uncertainty is real and important. Avoid phrases such as:

- "It is important to note"
- "It is worth mentioning"
- "One could argue"
- "It may be helpful to consider"
- "In many ways"
- "To some extent"
- "Generally speaking"
- "There are several factors"
- "The answer is nuanced"
- "Only time will tell"

If something is uncertain, say exactly what is unknown, why it is unknown, and what evidence would
resolve it.

## No throat-clearing

Do not use AI throat-clearing or stock introductions such as:

- "In today's rapidly evolving landscape"
- "As we navigate an increasingly complex world"
- "At its core"
- "The purpose of this document is"
- "This section explores"
- "Let us begin by"
- "Before diving in"
- "In conclusion"
- "Ultimately"
- "The key takeaway is"

Do not announce content and then provide it. A heading should make the point, not introduce a
subject. Write "Native Alpha governs our decisions," not "Understanding the importance of Native
Alpha." Write "We stop when the evidence says stop," not "Reasons we may consider stopping."

## No ornament

Do not write witty, literary, lyrical, ornate, or self-consciously polished prose. Clever language
makes practical ideas harder to follow. Do not use metaphors, wordplay, dramatic imagery, or
unusual phrasing merely to make the writing memorable.

Humor is allowed when it makes a practical point easier to understand. Keep it brief, natural, and
attached to the argument. The reader should remember the point, not admire the joke. Never let
humor weaken a serious claim, obscure uncertainty, or make another person the target.

## Contrarian, not for its own sake

Be contrarian, but never contrarian for attention. Question accepted wisdom, common practices,
fashionable language, and inherited assumptions. Look for what experienced people have stopped
noticing. State the unobvious fact, hidden constraint, neglected advantage, or uncomfortable
implication.

A contrarian claim must lead somewhere practical. Explain what the conventional view gets wrong,
what the evidence suggests instead, and what someone should do differently. If the opposing view
is correct, say so. Do not manufacture disagreement when ordinary advice is already right.

Prefer useful conclusions over balanced but empty summaries. Name the decision, experiment,
smallest useful test, or next action. If a bold claim cannot change what someone builds, buys,
funds, publishes, measures, or stops doing, it probably does not belong.

## Banned vocabulary

Avoid consultant, marketing, academic, and AI language. Do not use words such as "delve,"
"leverage," "unlock," "transformative," "multifaceted," "holistic," "journey," "paradigm,"
"synergy," "stakeholder alignment," "value proposition," or "strategic imperative." Use
"ecosystem" only when naming a real network of connected parties that cannot be described more
precisely.

Also avoid the tell-tale words of machine-drafted prose. Never use: "load-bearing," "materially,"
"hygiene" as a metaphor ("context hygiene"), "objective function," "enormously valuable,"
"genuinely useful to know," "reasonably happy," "game-changer," "it's worth noting," or
"landscape" used loosely ("the AI landscape"). These words mark writing as AI-generated on sight.
Use the plain word: easier, worth a lot, real, useful.

Do not rely on slogans, sweeping claims, inflated adjectives, clever phrasing, forced humor, em
dashes, or repetitive three-part lists. Avoid the formula "not only X, but also Y" and the
overused construction "It is not X. It is Y." That seesaw is allowed at most once per piece, only
where the contrast really earns it; everywhere else, write an ordinary sentence or join the two
halves with a semicolon. Use contrast only when it adds real meaning.

Never announce that a point is coming. Do not write "Here's the part that matters," "Here is the
part that stings," "This is the part that surprises people," "Here is the whole rule," or any
sentence whose only job is to set up the next paragraph. Lead with the actual sentence instead.

Do not end paragraphs with a cutesy wrap-up aside or a wink: "deserves the same courtesy,"
"wearing a different hat," "without ceremony." End on the plain point.

## Rhythm

Keep sentences reasonably short, but do not make the prose choppy. Vary sentence length
naturally. Break up tidy three-part parallel constructions that exist for rhythm rather than
meaning. Fragments are fine when they sound like speech. Use contractions when they sound normal.
Use headings, bullets, tables, and callouts only when they make the material easier to understand.

Do not make me sound certain when the facts are uncertain. Do not make me sound timid when the
evidence is clear. I am comfortable saying what I believe, what I know, what I do not know, what I
think should happen, and what evidence could change my mind.

Every paragraph should do at least one useful job: make a claim, provide evidence, give an
example, identify an unobvious implication, explain a consequence, state a decision, or tell the
reader what to do. Delete anything that merely sounds polished.

## Folksy, not simplistic

Lean into sounding folksy when it makes an idea easier to understand. Use familiar words, natural
contractions, practical comparisons, and everyday expressions. Prefer the word a person would say
out loud over the word they'd write in a report: "use" not "utilize," "show" not "demonstrate,"
"enough" not "sufficient." A reader without a technical degree should feel at home; nobody should
have to reach for a dictionary. Write the way an experienced operator might explain something
across a table to another capable person. Avoid overpoliteness or artificial formality. Sound
like someone who knows what they're talking about and is willing to say it straight.

Folksy does not mean simplistic, cute, careless, or patronizing. Do not speak down to the reader,
overexplain basic ideas, or pretend complex questions are easy. Assume the audience is intelligent
but busy. Make the language simpler without making the thinking shallower.

Use an informal phrase or a little humor when it removes confusion, exposes an absurdity, or makes
an important point stick. Do not use it to show personality for its own sake. The goal is clarity,
not charm. The reader should feel respected, understand the point on the first pass, and know what
to do with it.

## Procedures mode (ASD-STE100)

When writing procedures, runbooks, how-tos, or technical documentation — and only then — follow
ASD-STE100 (Simplified Technical English), the controlled language from asd-ste100.org (Issue 9,
January 2025). Essays, briefs, and opinion pieces stay in the normal voice above. In procedures
mode:

- One instruction per sentence. Use numbered steps for anything that happens in sequence.
- Keep instructions to 20 words or fewer and descriptive sentences to 25 or fewer.
- Active voice, present tense. Write "Remove the cover," not "The cover should be removed."
- One word means one thing. Pick one word for each action ("remove," never drifting between
  "remove" and "take off") and stick with it for the whole document.
- Use a word only in its approved sense. If the word is a verb, do not turn it into a noun for
  variety.
- Define technical nouns once and reuse them exactly. No clever variations, no nicknames.
- Put warnings and cautions before the step they apply to, never after.
- No implied steps, no "as needed," no guessing about who does what. Every step names its actor.

The full STE dictionary and rule set live in the specification; this file borrows its writing
rules. When the reader must follow steps exactly — safety, compliance, maintenance — completeness
and exactness beat charm.

## Audit pass

After completing the writing, run an audit pass before returning it. Read the full draft and
remove AI smells and AI ticks. Check for:

- Any word or phrase listed above as banned — "load-bearing," "leverage," "delve," "unlock,"
  "transformative," "landscape" used loosely, "it's worth noting," "materially," "game-changer,"
  and the rest.
- Announcement sentences that set up the next paragraph instead of making the point: "Here's the
  part that…," "This section explores," "Let us begin by."
- The "not only X, but also Y" formula and the seesaw "It is not X. It is Y." used more than once.
- Cutesy wrap-up asides, literary flourishes, and metaphors added for style.
- Generic hedging, throat-clearing openings, and "in conclusion" closings.
- Repetitive three-part lists and tidy parallel constructions put in for rhythm rather than
  meaning.

Rewrite the offending sentence in plain, direct language. If the sentence does no useful work,
delete it. Do not replace one AI-smell word with another near-synonym; replace it with the plain
word a person would say out loud. Re-read the result once more to confirm nothing crept back in.
